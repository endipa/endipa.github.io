Overview

For members: Discuss on Zulip This page in the wiki Propose a change TeX source PDF PDF of the whole wiki TeX of the whole wiki (zip)

A passive scalar is a quantity carried by a fluid without acting on it, such as temperature, a dye in water or a pollutant in the air. If \(f\) is its concentration, \(u\) the velocity of an incompressible flow, and \(\nu>0\) the molecular diffusivity, then \(f\) solves the advection–diffusion equation \[\partial_t f+u\cdot\nabla f=\nu\Delta f,\qquad \nabla\cdot u=0.\] Diffusion alone dissipates the scalar on a time scale of order \(1/\nu\), which is very long when \(\nu\) is small. The flow, on the other hand, dissipates nothing: it only rearranges the scalar. In doing so, it can transfer large-scale structures to small scales, for instance by stretching the scalar into thin filaments, and at small scales diffusion is much more efficient. The result is that \(f\) decays on a time scale much shorter than \(1/\nu\): this is enhanced dissipation.

How, and by how much, does an incompressible velocity field speed up the dissipation of a diffusing scalar?

The two videos below show the phenomenon in the simplest setting, a periodic box in two dimensions. In the first, a steady shear flow significantly accelerates the dissipation of vertical stripes, orthogonal to its streamlines.

The second video compares two flows of the same size. A steady shear stretches the scalar only along its own streamlines. Shears that alternate in direction, with random phases (the model of Pierrehumbert), stretch and fold the scalar, which then dissipates much faster and more uniformly in space.

How this was made →

Quantifying the time scale of enhanced dissipation is the subject of this wiki. Laminar flows, such as shear flows, typically dissipate the scalar on time scales of order \(\nu^{-a}\) with \(0<a<1\), whereas mixing flows, such as the alternating shears, can dissipate it on time scales of order \(|\log\nu|\). The questions are which time scales are possible, which flows achieve them, and how one proves it.

NoteMixing: the problem without diffusion

When \(\nu=0\) the equation is pure transport: the flow conserves every \(L^p\) norm of \(f\) and dissipates nothing. It can still mix, that is, move the scalar to ever smaller scales, so that \(f\) converges weakly to its mean while its \(L^2\) norm stays constant. The speed of mixing can be measured by the decay of a negative Sobolev norm, such as \(\|f\|_{H^{-1}}\). Mixing is a field of its own, with its own questions and methods; this wiki covers only its relation to enhanced dissipation.

NoteBeyond passive scalars

The same mechanism, acting on the vorticity of a two-dimensional fluid, helps keep shear flows stable at large Reynolds numbers, against perturbations that are small enough in terms of the Reynolds number; the page Why it matters expands on this.

NoteThe setting, made precise

In most of the wiki, the domains without boundary are the torus \(\mathbb{T}^2\) and the periodic strip \(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\); some problems are set in the whole plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\). The domains with boundary are the disk and the periodic channel \(\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]\); the boundary conditions are stated where they are used. The initial datum \(f_0\) is in \(L^2\) and has mean zero; for shear and circular flows, it also has zero average along each streamline, since the flow does not act on that average. The size of a solution is its \(L^2\) norm. One definition of the dissipation time of a flow is the time it needs to reduce every initial datum by a fixed factor, say one half; the flow enhances dissipation when this time is much shorter than \(1/\nu\). Definitions and notation are collected in Notation.
